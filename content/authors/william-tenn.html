Philip Klass was born in London in 1920, to a Jewish family of Russian immigrant roots, and carried across the Atlantic before he could speak. He was raised in the Brooklyn of East New York and Brownsville, the eldest of three children, absorbing the cadence of the city the way other children absorb air: the street-corner talk, the quick wit, the sharp eye for what people pretended not to be. He would spend his life rendering that talk, and the institutions that tried to govern it, into fiction.

He did not come to science fiction through the library door, though reading was present. He came through war. A combat engineer in Europe during the Second World War, he returned to work as a technical editor in an Air Force radar and radio laboratory, and later at Bell Labs. It was the radar that wrote his first story. Alexander the Bait, published in Astounding Science Fiction in May 1946, concerned a radar beam aimed at the Moon; within months, real-world radar achieved the same contact, and the premise became obsolete overnight. It was a beginning almost too perfect: a writer whose imagination kept pace with a reality that moved faster than prediction.

He published under the pseudonym William Tenn, a name he used for virtually all of his fiction, and under that name established himself as one of the sharpest comic voices the field had produced. The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction ranks him among “the genre’s very few genuinely comic, genuinely incisive writers of short fiction:” sharper and more mature than Fredric Brown, less self-indulgent than Robert Sheckley. His natural home was Galaxy Science Fiction, under the exacting editorship of H. L. Gold, where the era’s sharpest satires found their outlet.

His concerns were the machinery of human institutions: bureaucracy, power inversions, time paradoxes, the darker consequences of progress. Brooklyn Project took the paradox of time travel and aimed it at bureaucratic thought-control and the anti-Communist witch-hunts—a story John W. Campbell had rejected, and which Gold embraced. Child’s Play, The Liberation of Earth, Down Among the Dead Men all worked the same seam: take the apparatus of official reason seriously enough to take it apart, and let the dismantling look like laughter until it is too late. The surface humor was genuine; beneath it sat a pessimist’s caution.

His true scale was the short story, the form fitted to his gift for compression, for the surprise that arrives just as the reader relaxes. His book-length work was remarkably compact: two novels, Of Men and Monsters, grown from The Men in the Walls, in which humans have been reduced to vermin living in the walls of giant alien dwellings; and A Lamp for Medusa, alongside six collections assembled between 1955 and 1968. He wrote less as the decades advanced, and the stories arrived with increasing rarity.

His method reached one of its most elegant expressions in Venus Is a Man’s World, published in Galaxy in July 1951. Earth is dominated by women, who hold logic, government, and citizenship as their province; Venus is a male frontier where men vastly outnumber women, and a shipload of women travels there seeking husbands. The tale is told from the viewpoint of one young dependent male, Ferdinand Sparling, caught between the two worlds. The social order is turned and held up to the light, and the ending lands not as comedy but as an ironic judgment on biology and power. The story was later adapted for the radio series X Minus One, and its text remains freely available, still sharp after three-quarters of a century.

His influence ran deeper than his name recognition. Theodore Sturgeon regarded him as a foundational influence on satirical science fiction, and in 1966, without a college degree, Philip Klass accepted a one-year teaching appointment at Pennsylvania State University. He remained for more than two decades, teaching English, comparative literature, creative writing, and science fiction as literature. Among his students were David Morrell, creator of Rambo; the screenwriter Steven E. de Souza; the technology journalist Steven Levy; and the crime novelist Ray Ring. He taught the craft as he had practiced it: by indirection, by irony, by showing rather than telling.

Recognition came late, as it sometimes does to those who make their mark through humor. He was named SFWA Author Emeritus in 1999; his complete short fiction was assembled by NESFA Press in two volumes in 2001; he served as Guest of Honor at the 2004 Worldcon, where his nonfiction collection Dancing Naked was a Hugo finalist. He died in February 2010, in a Pittsburgh suburb, of congestive heart failure, at eighty-nine. His legacy is a paradox he would have appreciated: he made readers laugh, and he is remembered for what the laughter concealed: a quiet, running critique of the institutions that cannot quite be trusted, delivered with the lightest touch and the sharpest point.
